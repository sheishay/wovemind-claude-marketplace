# Evidence behind the rules

This file lists the research each page rule rests on, with the numbers that were checked. Where a source is given without a number, only the direction of the effect is claimed. Read it when the user asks why the page looks the way it does.

## How strong the evidence is overall

Noetel et al. (2022), Review of Educational Research, pulled together 29 reviews of multimedia design principles (https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329). The effects of these principles are smaller when learners control the pace themselves, as they do on a web page. Treat every rule below as a likely improvement, not a guarantee.

## Rules and their sources

**Every visual must carry content; no decoration, fun facts or story hooks.**
Interesting but irrelevant material ("seductive details") lowers learning: Rey (2012), Educational Research Review 7; Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), Educational Psychology Review 32 (https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4). One 2021 study had trouble reproducing the effect, so the direction is stated here without an effect size.

**Labels sit on the chart or diagram, not in a legend; text sits next to the picture it explains.**
Contiguity and split attention: Mayer (2021), Multimedia Learning, 3rd edition; Ginns (2006), Learning and Instruction. Placing related words and pictures together helps; making the reader look back and forth hurts. Direction only.

**One bottom line at the top, headings that state the idea, one main idea per section.**
Signaling, meaning cues that mark the structure and the main points, helps: Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016), r = .17 for transfer; Alpizar, Adesope & Wong (2020), d = .38.

**"Check yourself" prompts on concept pages.**
Practice testing is rated high utility and highlighting low utility: Dunlosky et al. (2013), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1). Self-explanation prompts help: Bisra et al. (2018), g = .55. Questions asked before the answer help the content they ask about (g = .54) but not other content (g = .04): St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn (2024), Psychonomic Bulletin & Review. This is why each prompt sits next to the idea it tests and the page has only one or two.

**Animation only as a reader-controlled stepper; nothing autoplays.**
Animation beats static pictures modestly overall: Höffler & Leutner (2007), Learning and Instruction, d = 0.37; Berney & Bétrancourt (2016), Computers & Education 101, g = 0.226. It clearly wins only when the specifics of a change must be learned: Ploetzner, Berney & Bétrancourt (2021). A stepper gives the reader the change frame by frame at their own pace.

**Interaction is limited to reveal, step and collapse.**
Giving learners more control over the material adds almost nothing: Karich, Burns & Maki (2014), g about 0.05. Open-ended interactivity costs effort without a measured gain.

**Bars and dots only, no pie, area or colour-only encodings.**
People judge position along a common scale most accurately, then length; angle, area and colour are judged worse: Cleveland & McGill (1984), Journal of the American Statistical Association 79; See also Heer & Bostock (2010), CHI.

**Flow diagrams and bespoke diagrams for structure and relationships.**
Studying node-and-link maps helps learning: Nesbit & Adesope (2006), Review of Educational Research 76; Schroeder et al. (2018), g = .43 for studying a map.

**A text column of about 65 characters per line.**
Lines of 55 characters beat lines of 100 characters: Dyson & Haselgrove (2001), International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 54. The evidence is modest, so the page uses a moderate width rather than an extreme one.

**No special fonts, Bionic Reading or highlighting tricks.**
No single font is best for everyone: Wallace et al. (2022), ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction 29(4). Bionic Reading showed no effect on reading: Acta Psychologica (2024), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38723450/. The page uses the reader's system font.

**Bottom line first, concise scannable text, and details collapsed until wanted.**
These come from usability practice rather than controlled trials. Concise, scannable and objective writing improved the usability of web pages in Morkes & Nielsen (1997), https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/. Bottom-line-first and progressive disclosure are standard practice built on that line of work.

## The picture-first layout

The page opens with a picture of the whole idea, shows a route of a few numbered stops, and leads each stop with a visual. The sources below support the direction of these choices; no effect sizes are claimed.

- **A plain, simple first screen.** Visual complexity lowers how appealing a website looks, and the judgement forms within the first 50 milliseconds: Tuch et al. (2012), https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/ . This is why the opening is one picture and one sentence, and why the system font and quiet styling stay.
- **A page that looks easy to read gets started.** Instructions in a font that is harder to read made a task seem longer and harder, and lowered willingness to do it: Song & Schwarz (2008), Psychological Science 19(10), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19000208/ .
- **Icon arrays for proportions.** A systematic review found that visual aids such as icon arrays help people understand proportions and risks: Garcia-Retamero & Cokely (2017), Human Factors.
- **Diagrams that show how ideas connect.** Graphic organizers beat outlines for learning the relations between ideas: Robinson & Kiewra (1995), Journal of Educational Psychology 87. Replications are mixed, so this is a direction, not a guarantee.
- **One idea per step, text linked to its visual.** A layout that showed one idea per step with linked text and visuals gave better comprehension than a plain vertical layout: Zhi, Ottley & Metoyer (2019), Computer Graphics Forum, https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf13719 .
- **A picture is not knowledge by itself.** An infographic was liked more than a plain text summary but produced no gain in knowledge: Buljan et al. (2018), Journal of Clinical Epidemiology 97. Every visual therefore has to carry content, which is why each block is built from the source's own points.

What each part aims at: the opening picture, the route strip and the stop count aim at willingness to start reading. Comprehension gains, where the research shows them, come from the blocks that show structure, quantity or sequence (chains, flows, icon arrays, charts, steppers), not from the opening picture. The line that tells the reader they may stop after the bottom line, and the progress cues (route marking and the scroll bar), have no direct trial behind them; they are kept because they are cheap and follow from the findings above.

## The ADHD-specific rules

Working memory mediates the reading comprehension difficulties seen in ADHD: Parks et al. (2022), Journal of Attention Disorders, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10870547211068047. Readers with ADHD also recall fewer of a text's central ideas: Yeari & Lavie (2021). That is the reason for stating the central idea outright, keeping visible groups small, and putting nothing important where it must be held in mind.

No controlled study tests headings, chunking, progress indicators or time estimates in ADHD adults. The ADHD-specific rules on this page (reading time shown, a progress bar while scrolling, tangents moved to a collapsed block, one concrete next action at the end) are therefore extrapolations from the working-memory research above and from the i-have-adhd output rules (https://github.com/ayghri/i-have-adhd, MIT licence).

## Included as reasonable practice, sources not verified

Two rules are kept because they are common, cheap and unlikely to hurt, but their sources were not verified for this skill:
- About four visible items per group.
- A concrete example before the general rule.
