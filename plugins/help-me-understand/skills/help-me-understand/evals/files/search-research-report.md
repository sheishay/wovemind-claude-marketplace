# Search engine evaluation for the product catalogue (findings so far)

So, picking up from the question in the planning thread about whether we should keep running product search on Postgres full-text search or move to a dedicated search engine, I spent most of last week on this and want to write down everything I found before I forget it, roughly in the order I found it, so apologies in advance that this is long. The short context is that the catalogue has about 2.4 million products today, growing at roughly 40,000 a month, and the complaints from the merchandising team are mainly about two things: typos returning nothing (someone types "sneekers" and gets an empty page), and search feeling slow on the category pages that combine a text query with three or four filters.

I looked at four options in the end: staying on Postgres full-text search with some tuning, Meilisearch, Typesense, and Elasticsearch (I also briefly looked at OpenSearch, but it is close enough to Elasticsearch for our purposes that I folded it in; the fork happened in 2021 after the licence change, which is an interesting story in itself, Elastic moved to the SSPL and AWS forked the last Apache-licensed version, and then in 2024 Elastic added AGPL as an option again, though none of that changes much for us since we would self-host either way, or possibly use a managed offering, which I will come back to).

On latency, which was the main thing I measured: I loaded a copy of the full catalogue into each engine on the same size of machine (4 vCPU, 16 GB RAM) and replayed a sample of 50,000 real queries from last month's logs, including the filter combinations. The p95 latency for Postgres, after I added the GIN index and tuned the ranking query as far as I reasonably could, was 180 ms. Meilisearch came in at 22 ms, Typesense at 18 ms, and Elasticsearch at 35 ms. It is worth saying that the Postgres number gets a lot worse on the filter-heavy category pages, where I saw p95 of around 410 ms, whereas the three dedicated engines stayed under 50 ms on those pages, which is really the core of the complaint.

Typo tolerance is the other big one. Postgres full-text search has no typo tolerance out of the box; you can bolt on the pg_trgm extension for trigram similarity, and I tried it, but combining trigram matching with ranking and filters made the queries noticeably slower again (p95 went from 180 ms to about 260 ms), and the results were still worse than the dedicated engines on misspellings. All three dedicated engines handle typos by default. In my misspelling test set of 300 queries, Typesense and Meilisearch both returned a relevant first result for about 94 percent of them, and Elasticsearch for about 89 percent with its default fuzzy settings, though I suspect that gap would close with tuning.

Cost, assuming we self-host on our existing cloud account: Postgres is effectively zero extra since we already run it. Meilisearch would be about 60 dollars a month for a suitably sized instance, Typesense about 75 dollars a month (it keeps the whole index in RAM, so it needs a bit more memory), and Elasticsearch about 410 dollars a month, because a production setup really wants three nodes for resilience. The managed offerings roughly double those numbers, except Elastic Cloud, which is closer to triple.

Operational effort matters here because there are only two of us on the platform side. Meilisearch and Typesense are both single binaries and were each running in under an hour. Elasticsearch took most of a day to set up properly, needs JVM heap tuning, and has more moving parts to monitor. Any of the three dedicated engines means we have to keep the search index in sync with Postgres, which I would do with the existing outbox table and a small worker; I estimate about three days of work for that sync, whichever engine we choose, and it is the same work for all three.

A few other things I noticed along the way. Elasticsearch is clearly the most powerful for analytics-style queries and aggregations, and if we ever need facet counts across more than about 10 million documents, or complex relevance tuning with custom scoring scripts, it is the only one of the three I would trust at that scale. Meilisearch has the nicest dashboard, which the merchandising team liked when I showed them. Typesense supports vector search and geo search out of the box, which matters a little because the store-locator project next year will want geo queries. One caveat about Meilisearch: some of its features, such as the analytics, are only in the cloud version, and its licence for the core engine is MIT, but the enterprise edition is separate, so we would want to check exactly which features we need before committing. Typesense is GPL-3.0, which is fine for us because we would run it as a separate service and not distribute it.

Something I have not resolved: none of the engines support our current "boost products with stock in the customer's nearest warehouse" rule natively, so that would either need a precomputed field per warehouse in the index (there are 14 warehouses) or a re-ranking step in our API. I have not measured the cost of either yet.

Also, slightly off-topic, but the Postgres ranking function we use was written in 2019 and nobody remembers why it weights the brand field three times higher than the title; that might be worth revisiting separately regardless of what we decide.

So, putting all of that together, and I realise I should have led with this: my recommendation is Typesense. It had the best latency and joint-best typo handling, it costs about 75 dollars a month, it was quick to run, and its geo search fits next year's store-locator project. Meilisearch is a very close second and would also be a fine choice; the main reasons I lean away from it are the cloud-only features and the lack of built-in geo search on the version I tested. I would only choose Elasticsearch if the catalogue were heading past 10 million products or we needed heavy aggregations, and I would only stay on Postgres if we decided the typo problem was not worth three days of sync work plus a new service. The thing I would do first is run Typesense against the real query log for a week in shadow mode, comparing its results with the live Postgres results, before switching any traffic.
